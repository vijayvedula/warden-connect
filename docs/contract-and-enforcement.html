The ceiling and
the checkpoint

An agent calls a tool it was never meant to reach. Afterwards, every control you bought reports green. This is how a contract and an enforcement point become one control — and why either half alone is decoration.

01

One call. Two owners. No decision.

A reconciliation agent asks a payments service to move money. The call succeeds. Who agreed that it could?

Not the payments team — nobody asked them about this particular caller. Not the agent's team — they asked for a broad credential once, a year ago, and got it. Not the network, which saw two internal addresses and did its job. Not the identity provider, which authenticated a service account and never had an opinion about tools.

Each layer behaved correctly. The decision that mattered — may this caller reach this capability on this callee — was never made by anyone, because no layer is where that decision lives.

02

The token is not the agreement

The obvious repair is to tighten the credential. It does not reach, and the reason is organisational rather than technical.

A token is issued by an identity administrator to a workload. It asserts identity, and a class of permitted activity. But the person who grants it has never read the callee's tool list, does not know which of those tools moves money, and is not accountable when one of them does. Scope is coarse because the issuer is far from the data.

MechanismWhat it actually assertsWho decides
Network policythese two addresses may exchange packetsplatform
mTLS / SPIFFEthe peer is who it claims to beplatform
OAuth scopethis workload may do things of this kindidentity admin
API gateway routethis path is reachable and rate-limitedplatform
Runtime policyunder these conditions, denysecurity
—this caller may reach these tools on this calleenobody

Every row is a real control and each is worth having. The gap in the last row is not a product gap in any of them — it is that the assertion requires two parties to agree, and none of these mechanisms has a place to record a two-party agreement.

the shape of the missing thing It must be authored by the two owners, not by a central admin. It must be reviewable before it takes effect. It must be legible to a machine at call time. And it must not be able to grant anything — or you have built a second, parallel entitlement system, and made the problem worse.
03

Two declarations, one agreement

The provider writes down what it offers and on what terms. The consumer writes down what it needs. Where those overlap, and only there, a contract exists.

Both declarations are files in the two teams' own repositories — the provider's offer.toml, the consumer's needs.toml. Which means the approval is not a workflow anyone has to adopt. It is a pull request, reviewed by the owner who already reviews that repository, and the audit trail is a merge commit that was going to exist anyway.

what changes for governance Approving a connection stops being a ticket queue and becomes the review your engineers already do. The reviewer is the owner of the thing being exposed, because it is their repository. Nobody has to be trained on a new tool, and the evidence an auditor wants — who approved what, when — is the merge history.
04

The algebra, and the property that matters

What a call is permitted to do is the intersection of three independent ceilings. Not a sum, not a precedence order — an intersection.

effective = contract.surface ∩ token.scope ∩ policy_decision three ceilings; the lowest one wins, every time

Because it is an intersection, adding a contract to a system cannot make anything newly possible. The most permissive contract anyone could write is bounded above by what the token and the policy engine already allowed. A mistaken contract narrows the wrong thing or fails to narrow — it never opens a door.

That is the property that makes the delegation safe. You can hand contract authoring to two hundred product teams without giving two hundred teams the ability to grant themselves access, because the artifact they author has no grant semantics at all. It is a ceiling. Its only power is downward.

the question to ask a vendor Can the thing your teams author ever increase what is possible? If yes, you have bought a second entitlement system that must now be governed as tightly as the first. If no, you have bought a constraint, and constraints compose.
switch a ceiling off and watch what becomes reachable
what recon-bot can reach on payments-mcp

Switch off token.scope and nothing changes — the contract was already the tighter ceiling. That non-event is the clearest picture of what a ceiling is: removing a constraint can only ever widen, and a term that was not binding was never doing work. Switch off contract.surface and money movement appears, because the contract was the only term that knew this caller had no business moving money.

05

Now switch off the checkpoint

The fourth switch is different in kind from the other three, and it is the whole reason this page exists.

Turn it off and the expression does not shrink or widen. It stops being evaluated. The contract is still signed. It is still merged, still in the audit trail, still on the slide that says connections are governed. The compliance evidence is genuine and the control is imaginary.

This is the failure mode worth being afraid of, because it is invisible from every direction an executive normally looks. There is no alert. Coverage dashboards count contracts, and the contracts exist. The only signal that distinguishes an enforced contract from a decorative one is a refusal — and a system that is refusing nothing looks exactly like a system with nothing to refuse.

the recurring defect class A control that reads as configured and does nothing. Not a bug in the check — the check is correct and well tested. The wiring simply never routes a call through it: a filter declared but not attached to a listener, a policy loaded but keyed on a field that is always absent, a verifier that fails open on a timeout it never logs.

The operational consequence is a single question you can ask about any deployment, and it admits only one good answer:

“Show me a call this refused today.” a coverage number is a claim; a refusal is evidence
06

Where you put the check decides who can remove it

There are three places to evaluate the expression, and they differ far less in what they can see than in who holds the switch.

This is an ownership question wearing a topology costume. A check that runs inside the caller's process is a check the caller's team can disable, redeploy without, or simply fail to adopt — and the callee's owner, who is accountable for the data, has no way to know.

 Caller identity fromWho can bypassRollout costUse when
A · in-processits own configuration — self-assertedthe caller’s team, silentlynone — a dependencythe caller is the party you are protecting, e.g. an agent you want to keep inside its lane
B · in the paththe peer certificate — unforgeable by the callerthe platform team, visiblya proxy already in the paththe default for internal traffic
C · in the calleethe presented credentialthe callee’s teamevery server must adopt the libraryno proxy exists, or the decision needs application context

Choosing B is not a claim that callers are adversaries. It is a claim that assurance which depends on every team's continued cooperation is not assurance you can report upward. The platform already terminates mTLS and already knows both peers; putting the evaluation there costs one filter and gives the callee's owner something they can rely on without auditing anyone else's deployment.

the pragmatic position A and B are not exclusive, and the honest combination is to run B for assurance and A for developer feedback — the in-process check fails fast at the caller with a good error message, and the in-path check is the one you count.
07

The thing you agreed to keeps moving

A contract names tools on a callee. On Tuesday the callee ships a fourth tool. Nothing about the contract changed, and everything about it is now wrong.

This is the failure that distinguishes a contract from a firewall rule. A rule about ports stays true because ports do not acquire new meanings. A rule about capabilities is a rule about an evolving surface, and the surface belongs to a team shipping every week.

The repair is that the contract records a cryptographic digest of the surface it was agreed against, and the enforcement point recomputes that digest from what the server actually serves. Drift is then not a monitoring problem to be noticed later. It is a refusal, now.

what this costs the provider team Changing the surface means re-publishing the offer and re-minting affected contracts — a pull request, not a negotiation. That friction is the control. Teams that resent it are telling you the surface changes more often than anyone downstream is being told, which is the thing you wanted to find out.
08

One line decides whether you have a control at all

The enforcement point depends on inputs: contracts on disk, a key set, a policy file. When one is unreachable, does traffic flow?

Every proxy has this setting and most ship permissive, because a security filter that can take production down is a hard sell in the room where it gets adopted. That default converts your control into a control-shaped object that disappears under exactly the conditions where it matters — incidents, restarts, partial outages, the moments when unusual calls happen.

When the checker cannot decideFail openFail closed
Traffic during the faultflows, uncheckedrefused
What the dashboard showsgreen — no denialsan incident
Who finds outnobodyeverybody, in minutes
Worst casean unbounded window with no control and no recordan outage with a clear cause

Fail-closed is the correct default and it is genuinely more expensive: it makes the enforcement point a dependency of every call, which means it needs the availability engineering of one. Budget for that, or accept that you have chosen fail-open and say so out loud, in the document where the control is claimed.

the second question to ask What happens to traffic when the enforcement point cannot reach its inputs? If the answer takes more than one sentence, or if the person answering has to go and look, the behaviour under fault has never been tested — and it is the only behaviour that decides what the control is worth.
09

The honest ledger

What follows is what this model does and does not do, stated the way you would want it stated if you were the one accountable for the claim.

ClaimStanding
Two-party contract, authored in git, approved by reviewed mergeRun end to end — live source control, two repositories, two merges, a provider-approved gated term
Enforcement in the network path, identity from the peer certificateRun end to end — a real proxy admitting contracted calls and refusing six distinct ways
Surface drift refuses, and revokes what was already verifiedRun end to end
Rate ceilings per contractImplemented and tested; not load-proven
Spend ceilings — a cost cap per dayCannot bind. Nothing on the path knows what a call costs. It is expressible in an offer and unenforceable at the checkpoint; treat it as documentation until a cost signal exists
More than one contract between the same two partiesThe second is verified, counted, and unreachable at a gateway — resolution is by party pair. A known limit, not a subtlety
External callers and external calleesOut of scope by design. A contract process presumes both parties follow it; partners do not. Perimeter controls own those flows

Two of those rows exist because someone followed the setup guide end to end and hit them. Both were the defect class this page names in beat 05 — a second contract that verified, counted, and enforced nothing, and three unrelated refusal paths that all reported the same bare code, making the real cause undiagnosable. Neither was visible from the tests. Both were visible within minutes of a person doing the whole thing in order.

What it costs, against what it bounds

CostOrder of magnitude
Added latency per callone in-process filter hop; sub-millisecond against the network round trip it rides along with
New failure domainreal — fail-closed makes it a dependency of every contracted call
Per-connection human efforttwo pull requests, reviewed by owners who already review those repositories
Per-surface-change effortone pull request, plus re-minting affected contracts
Platform effortone filter in a proxy that is already in the path, plus a control plane

Against that: for every internal agent-to-service connection, a named owner approved a named capability list, a machine refuses everything outside it, and the evidence of both is ordinary version control history. The bound is not that nothing goes wrong. It is that when something does, the set of things it could have reached was written down in advance, by the person accountable for it.

A contract is a claim about what should be possible.
An enforcement point is the only thing that turns it into a fact.
