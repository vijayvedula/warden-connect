The core loop: approve the relationship, not every call

This is the use case everything else supports. A developer asks for two tools on one server; a human approves once; and from then on the boundary is enforced by an artifact rather than by a process.

The flow

Each step is labelled with which layer owns it. The badge matters more than it looks: most of the confusion about this system is people expecting one layer to answer the other's question.

1
warden-connect

Request

Both parties, the exact tools, and why. Policy answers instantly whether it auto-approves: internal, read-only, unremarkable callee, and no human is needed.

2
warden-connect

Policy evaluates

Structural preconditions first, which no rule may override — then the zone bar, then the first matching rule, then the standing caps, which may only downgrade an allow to require-approval.

3
warden-connect

Approve

A detached signature from a named human's key — not a row in a table an operator with database access could write. Tier 1 needs two distinct approvers.

4
warden-connect

Mint

One signed artifact per mediator, carrying cid, the pinned hashes, the surface, the terms, and a hard exp. Never multi-audience: that would be replayable across enforcement points.

5
warden-connect

Distribute

Mediators pull and acknowledge exactly what they applied. A lost push is silent; a missed pull shows up as ACK lag an operator can alert on.

6
warden-connect

Verify at the mediator

Eleven checks: signature, expiry, audience, revocation, both peer identities, the surface pin, posture, the zone pair, the token binding.

7
warden-connect

Filter tools/list

The server offers three tools; the agent sees two. The model never learns wire_funds exists, so it cannot be manipulated into attempting it. This is prevention rather than detection.

8
Warden core

Enforce each call

Inside the surface, core decides every tools/call — scope, policy, ceilings, approvals. Two independent layers, neither able to widen the other.

9
both

Record with cid

Every action carries the connection id, so the relationship and the actions taken under it reconcile without anybody stitching logs together.

03

Where the boundary is

Two layers, and neither can widen the other. Removing one does not silently loosen what remains.

warden-connect

The relationship boundary

May these two parties be introduced at all, over what surface, on what terms, for how long. Answered once, recorded as a signed artifact, and enforced by the mediator's filter.

surface ∩ terms ∩ exp
Warden core

The action boundary

May this specific call proceed right now. Scope, policy, budget, held approvals — evaluated per call, inside a surface it did not choose.

token.scope ∩ policy_decision
04

The exception paths

Where the real design lives. A main flow describes the day everything works; these describe the days that decide whether anyone keeps the thing switched on.

A1Requested surface exceeds the declared surface

Rejected at request time with a precise diff. A contract is a ceiling, never a grant — it cannot conjure capability the callee never offered.

A2A cross-zone request

Escalated to the zone-crossing path. Crossings between trust levels are denied until explicitly declared, and the declaration is directional: permitting egress to a partner does not permit that partner to reach back.

A3Approval not granted within the SLA

The request expires and nothing is provisioned. Silence terminates; it never grants.

A4Hash mismatch at connect time

Refused on the spot, and drift is raised. The pin is checked at tools/list — and the first tools/call triggers the mediator's own listing, so skipping discovery cannot skip verification.

A5Contract expired mid-workload

The connection stops. There is no implicit grace: an expiry that quietly extends is not an expiry.

A6A ceiling breach

Deny the call, notify the owner, keep the contract valid. A rate spike is not grounds to tear down a relationship.
