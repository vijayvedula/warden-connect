Introducing warden-connect

The Two Layers

Two layers, asking two different questions.

One of them you already have.

Warden answers: may this specific call proceed, right now?

Session tokens and scope, per-call policy, held approvals, audit rows. The action boundary — decided every single call.

warden-connect answers what Warden never asks: may these two parties be introduced at all, over what surface, for how long?

The relationship boundary — decided once, and it arrives as five capabilities.

One. Pin the tool surface.

A hash over exactly what was contracted, so a silent change is detected rather than trusted.

Two. Know what you actually have.

Agents and services, with named owners, in a tamper-evident log.

Three. Turn an approval into an artifact.

Signed, expiring, machine-checkable. Not a ticket.

Four. Put the contract on the path.

Only contracted tools are visible; everything else is refused before the service is called.

Five. Contain the whole estate in one verb.

With per-node proof — and honest reporting when a node cannot confirm.

Each capability is independently valuable. You can stop on any of them.

And neither layer can ever widen the other. Which leaves the question this film is about — why does any of it need to exist?

The Connection

An agent needs to read a customer's balance.

So someone connects it to the payments service.

But look at what that connection actually is.

A credential copied from a wiki page. No expiry. No record anywhere that it exists.

Now do that four hundred times.

Different teams, different quarters, nobody keeping a list.

Which of these is allowed to exist?

Nobody can answer. There is no list to answer from.

The Obvious Fix

The obvious answer is to put a gate in front of it.

But the gate costs three weeks and an unclear approver —

and hands out a shared credential at the end of it anyway.

So engineers route around it. Quietly, and quite reasonably.

A control that can be bypassed is a control that will be.

Which leaves exactly one question worth asking.

What if the control made them faster?

The Idea

Start again, with one connection.

Don't try to govern every call.

Govern the relationship.

Write down what it is allowed to be.

This is not permission to act.

It is a ceiling on what acting could possibly mean.

Every layer can only narrow the one above it.

Remove any of them and the set gets smaller — never larger.

On the Path

Now put it where the traffic actually is.

The service offers nine tools. The agent is shown two.

The wrong tool cannot be called by accident, because it is not in the list.

And a call outside the contract is refused

before the service is ever spoken to.

Four hundred connections again —

but now every one has an owner, a scope and an expiry date.

So when one goes bad, containment is a single command.

Twelve of thirteen confirmed in forty-one seconds — and the thirteenth says it could not confirm, rather than reporting success.

six teams, one object

The same artifact, read five different ways

This is the property that makes it a control plane rather than a tool. Nobody has to adopt anybody else's workflow — they agree on one object, and each reads it for what they need.

What this is, stated plainly

A signed, expiring contract for every agent-to-service relationship — enforced in the request path, and revocable across the estate in under a minute with per-node proof that it landed.

It arrives in five steps. Each one is independently valuable, and you can stop on any of them indefinitely. The first two need no infrastructure, no budget line and no approval, which is why one team can prove the whole model in a week.

What it deliberately does not do

It does not replace identity, authorisation or network controls. It adds the relationship boundary those layers do not cover, and it cannot widen them.

Deployed in visibility-only mode it enforces nothing — and says so, loudly, rather than reporting a green status. That is the principle underneath all of it: a control that reports success while doing nothing is worse than no control, because nobody investigates something that looks fine.

The decision is small. One team, one week, and the estate gets a register with owners that can prove it was not edited afterwards.
